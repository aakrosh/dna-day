So — What Exactly Is DNA?

🧬

The Blueprint

DNA holds all the instructions that make you you — eye color, height, everything biological.

❄️

Unique to You

Except for identical twins, no two people share the same DNA. It's a biological fingerprint in every cell.

🔬

Left Everywhere

When you touch something, you leave skin cells, hair roots, or saliva — all carrying your full DNA profile.

⚖️

Used in Court

Since the 1980s, DNA evidence has solved thousands of crimes and freed many wrongly convicted people.

Your DNA Is Incredible

🍌

You share 60% of your DNA with a banana. And about 85% with a mouse.

📏

If you unraveled the DNA from just one cell, it would stretch about 6 feet — yet it fits inside something invisible to the eye.

👯

Every person shares 99.9% of their DNA with every other human. That 0.1% is what makes you unique.

🔢

Your genome has roughly 3 billion "letters" of code. Reading one per second would take 95 years.

Reading DNA Evidence

Gel electrophoresis sorts DNA fragments by size. An electric current pushes them through a gel — smaller pieces travel farther, larger ones stay near the top.

The result is a pattern of glowing bands — like a barcode unique to each person. Compare the crime scene pattern to each suspect's lane.

⚠ One suspect's bands will look very close to the scene — but close isn't the same as matching. Look carefully at the exact position of every band.

🔬 The Science

All four of Pippi's bands aligned exactly with the crime scene. Pooh's third band was shifted — a different-sized fragment, so not a match despite looking close. In real cases, scientists check 13+ locations. The odds of a false match? Less than 1 in a trillion.

This Is What We Do in Medical Genetics

🏥

The Question

Why do some people develop a disease — like a heart condition or cancer — while others don't? The answer is often hidden somewhere in their DNA.

👥

Compare Two Groups

Scientists read the entire genome — all 3 billion letters — from thousands of patients with a disease, and thousands of people without it. Then they look for locations where the letters differ.

💻

Computers Do the Searching

No human could scan 3 billion letters by hand. We write programs that scan every location across every genome and flag the spots that keep showing up in patients but not in healthy people.

🎯

Find the Culprit

Just like matching Pippi's bands to the crime scene — we're matching patterns to find which DNA location is linked to the disease, so we can understand it and eventually treat it.

The same idea as today's mystery — just with billions of letters, millions of people, and a lot more computing power.

Cystic Fibrosis — What Happens?

🫁

The Lungs

Normally, a thin layer of fluid lines the airways, trapping dust and bacteria so they can be cleared. In CF, this mucus becomes thick and sticky, clogging the airways and making it very hard to breathe.

🦠

Infections

Thick mucus is a perfect breeding ground for bacteria. CF patients suffer repeated, severe lung infections throughout their lives, which gradually damage the lungs over time.

🍽️

Digestion

The same sticky mucus blocks ducts in the pancreas, stopping digestive enzymes from reaching the gut. Many CF patients cannot absorb nutrients properly from food without enzyme supplements.

🧬

The Root Cause

All of this comes from a single broken protein called CFTR, which normally acts as a channel to control salt and water flow across cell surfaces. When CFTR doesn't work, the fluid balance fails — and the mucus turns thick.

CF affects about 1 in 2,500 people. Until recently, most patients did not survive past their 30s. Today, new treatments are changing that — and it all started with finding the mutation.

How Was F508del Found?

1989

Lap-Chee Tsui and Francis Collins mapped chromosome 7 and sequenced the CFTR gene — the first time a disease gene was found by systematic chromosome walking rather than knowing the protein first. A landmark moment in genetics.

The find

Comparing the CFTR sequence in CF patients vs. healthy people revealed that ~70% of CF patients were missing exactly 3 letters of DNA code — the letters that encode a single amino acid called phenylalanine at position 508.

Why it matters

Without those 3 letters, the CFTR protein folds incorrectly, gets flagged as defective by the cell, and is destroyed before it can reach the cell surface. No protein → no channel → thick mucus.

2023

The drug Trikafta (elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor) was developed specifically to fix the folding problem caused by F508del. It rescues the protein, gets it to the cell surface, and keeps it working. Median life expectancy has roughly doubled in the last 20 years.

The name F508del means: Phenylalanine (F) at position 508, deleted (del). Three missing letters. One broken protein. One disease.

What Did We Learn?

1

DNA is in almost every cell — and you're leaving traces of it everywhere, all the time.

2

Gel electrophoresis sorts DNA by fragment size, creating a barcode pattern scientists can compare.

3

Close isn't enough. One shifted band means no match — real forensics uses 13+ sites to eliminate any doubt.

4

More sites reveal more than identity. By reading thousands of locations across the genome, scientists can learn about disease risk, ancestry, inherited traits — and even predict things like eye color or height from DNA alone.
